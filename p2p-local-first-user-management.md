# Managing Users in P2P / Local-First Collaboration Apps: Invites, Revocation, Membership, Recovery, and Adversary Protection

## TL;DR
- The building blocks for user management in P2P/local-first team apps now exist as real libraries — capability/signature-chain membership (@localfirst/auth, p2panda-auth, Keyhive), decentralised group key agreement (BeeKEM, FREEK/DMLS), password-authenticated recovery (Kintsugi), and pairing-style invites (Keet's blind-pairing, Delta Chat SecureJoin) — but almost all of the *cryptographically-enforced, concurrency-tolerant* pieces are pre-alpha research code, while the production-ready systems are either server-mediated or make deliberate simplifications.
- Two architectural decisions are effectively irreversible and must be made up front: (1) the **forward-secrecy vs concurrency-tolerance** tradeoff (BeeKEM's κ parameter makes this explicit — any κ>1 kills forward secrecy but you need κ>1 to survive network partitions), and (2) **who may add/remove members and how concurrent conflicts resolve** (free delegation à la UCAN/Keyhive vs manager-only/strong-removal à la p2panda). Both leak into your data model and cannot be cleanly retrofitted.
- For an AWS-first architect building on Commonware/Rust: the pragmatic 2026 design is a signed, hash-chained membership log (the Keybase→@localfirst/auth→p2panda lineage) for authorisation, a CGKA (BeeKEM or DMLS) for read-encryption key rotation, multi-device as the primary recovery path with a BIP39/passkey fallback, iroh for transport/discovery, and an explicit decision to treat "is this human who they claim" as **out of scope** for the crypto layer — because every shipping system does exactly that.

## Key Findings

**1. There is no turnkey "P2P user management" library — you assemble 4–5 layers.** The stack decomposes into: identity (keypairs/DIDs) → membership & authorisation (who is in, who may change it) → key agreement (read-encryption key rotation) → transport/discovery → storage. These are only *partly* separable. Membership and key agreement are tightly entangled in the strongest designs (Keyhive fuses them; p2panda deliberately keeps its access-control CRDT separate from key agreement so you can mix and match). Transport (iroh, Hyperswarm) is cleanly separable from all of the above.

**2. The membership problem is fundamentally a Byzantine-consistency problem, and the frontier is "finality."** The core hard case is the "duelling admins" problem: two admins concurrently revoke each other. Recent work (Kegan Dougal's ERA, Element/Matrix; Ink & Switch's Keyhive; p2panda's "strong removal" resolver) converges on the insight that pure CRDT convergence is *not enough* for security — you need either a conservative deterministic resolver or a notion of epoch/finality, because a Byzantine admin can exploit concurrency to win the race.

**3. The recovery problem is genuinely close to solved for the "lost device, kept password/passkey" case, and genuinely unsolved for the fully-decentralised "lost everything" case.** Multi-device is the honest primary answer everywhere. Beyond that, the spectrum runs from centralised HSM-backed (Signal SVR3) to decentralised threshold schemes (Kintsugi) to pure self-custody (BIP39 mnemonic / Nostr nsec, where losing the key means permanent loss).

**4. Every shipping system deliberately declines to solve identity assurance, moderation, and spam.** Delta Chat explicitly chose to ignore infiltrators and focus on transport attacks because the web-of-trust mental model is too hard for users. This is the single most important expectation to set with enterprise stakeholders.

## Details

### A. The systems, on shared axes

Below, "library-usable" means you can adopt it as a dependency without adopting a whole product; "production" distinguishes shipped systems from research prototypes.

#### Keyhive / BeeKEM / Beelay (Ink & Switch)
- **What it is:** A local-first access-control system, led by Brooklyn Zelenka. Three crates: `keyhive_core` (signing, encryption, delegation), `beelay-core` (auth-enabled encrypted sync), `keyhive_wasm` (TS bindings). "Documents are groups": every document has a dynamic member group, groups can contain groups (transitive membership), authorisation is via **convergent capabilities** / delegation chains.
- **Invites:** Capability delegation — you delegate access to a member's pre-shared public key.
- **Revocation:** Remove a member → the document's BeeKEM shared secret is rotated (PCS). Concurrency-tolerant by construction.
- **Membership model:** Delegation-based; free delegation (any member with sufficient capability can delegate onward), converging on a capability graph.
- **Key agreement:** **BeeKEM**, a decentralised CGKA adapted from TreeKEM. Crucially it exposes a **security parameter κ** = number of individual secrets each user retains, which trades forward secrecy against tolerance to concurrency. Any κ>1 eliminates forward secrecy, but κ>1 is *required* to survive network partitions (a member who Updated under partition otherwise loses access to concurrently-created group secrets). BeeKEM in isolation offers forward secrecy; **Keyhive as a whole does not**, because "causal encryption" means access to a chunk grants the key to the previous chunk.
- **Adversary model / formal analysis:** BeeKEM defends against **cross-fork attacks** (compromising a user on one branch must not yield group secrets from a concurrent branch) — an attack that *is* possible against Causal TreeKEM and Weidner et al.'s scheme. Formalised by Derek Yen (NYU Courant) et al. in **IACR ePrint 2026/1434** (linked from Lab Note 06, published 2026-07-31).
- **Maturity:** **Pre-alpha, no security audit, unstable APIs** (their own words). Rust. Latest public lab note is 06 (2026-07-31); the notebook stops there.
- **Fit:** This is the most conceptually complete design for exactly your use case (Automerge/CRDT team collaboration), in Rust, but not production-ready.

#### @localfirst/auth (+ CRDX) — Herb Caudill
- **What it is:** TypeScript library for "decentralized authentication and authorization for team collaboration, using a secure chain of cryptographic signatures." Every member keeps a full replica of the team's **signature chain** (hash-chained, signed actions), which doubles as a self-contained PKI/CA. State (members, roles, public keys) is computed from the chain. Built on **CRDX**, an extracted "encrypted, authenticated CRDT with bespoke conflict resolution."
- **Invites:** **Seitan token exchange** — an invitation is effectively an asymmetric-PAKE: the inviter derives a signature keypair from a secret invitation code, hands the code to the invitee over a second channel and the public key to the team; the invitee proves possession by signing. Explicitly designed so users never see cryptographic complexity. Uses **lockboxes** for delivering secrets to members and a **key-rotation DAG**.
- **Revocation:** Recorded as a signed action on the chain; key rotation via lockboxes.
- **Membership model:** Role-based (admins), all authorisations trace back to the founder's root action.
- **Transport:** BYO socket; demo uses `@localfirst/relay` (tiny WebSocket bridge).
- **Maturity:** The most mature *dedicated* team-membership library in this space; there is a documented aspiration (`automerge-repo-auth-localfirstauth`) to make it an Automerge Repo auth provider. TypeScript, not Rust.

#### p2panda-auth / p2panda-encryption
- **What it is:** Rust crate (`p2panda-auth`, v0.6.0, MIT/Apache-2.0), "decentralised group management with fine-grained, per-member permissions." An **eventually-consistent authorisation CRDT** — the first of its kind they shipped. Design write-ups: p2panda.org 2025-07-28 (design) and 2025-08-27 (learnings).
- **Access levels (a clean four-level model worth stealing):** **Pull** (replicate encrypted data, can't decrypt), **Read** (decrypt), **Write** (mutate), **Manage** (mutate group state).
- **Membership model:** **Manager-only** by default — only operations authored by members with "manage" access are applied. This is the deliberate contrast to Keyhive's free delegation.
- **Concurrency resolution:** A pluggable **`Resolver` trait**. The default is a cautious **"strong removal"** approach (a removal wins over concurrent operations by the removed member); you can implement custom resolvers (e.g. "freeze group state on conflict" vs "auto-promote all to manager").
- **Composition:** Deliberately *separate* from key agreement (`p2panda-encryption`), so you can combine the authz CRDT with different encryption strategies. Their capability system is UCAN-inspired but explicitly **not** UCAN-interoperable.
- **Fit:** The best-matched *production-track Rust* library for your goals; more conservative and simpler than Keyhive.

#### UCAN (1.0) + ucanto / rs-ucan
- **What it is:** User-Controlled Authorization Network — a capability token format. In 1.0 it split into separate **delegation**, **invocation**, and **revocation** specs (all active on GitHub through mid-2026; `rs-ucan` is the Rust implementation).
- **Invites/delegation:** Delegation is offline-capable and instant — you sign a token attenuating (never amplifying) your capabilities to a delegate.
- **Revocation:** By **CID** of the delegation being revoked. Must be signed by an issuer in the delegation chain (the storacha/w3up ecosystem found this too restrictive and added a `ucan/revoke` *capability* so revocation power can itself be delegated to auditors). Revocations are immutable, monotonically-growing, irreversible. **"Delegation is instant, revocation is eventually consistent"** and is explicitly "the last line of defence against abuse" — proactive expiry (time bounds) is preferred.
- **Membership model:** Not a membership system per se — a capability layer you build membership on top of.
- **Fit:** Good mental model and a usable token layer; but revocation latency semantics mean you must lean on short expiries, which fights offline-first.

#### Meadowcap / Willow (+ Earthstar, willow-rs)
- **What it is:** A capability system (Meadowcap) for the Willow data model, which organises data in a 3-dimensional space of **subspace × path × timestamp**. Rust implementation in `willow-rs`; TS in `meadowcap-js`; Earthstar is the application layer.
- **Capabilities:** Unforgeable signed tokens granting read or write, **delegable and restrictable** by subspace, path, and/or timestamp. Recursive verification.
- **Two namespace models (the key design choice):** **Communal** namespaces (authority derived from owning a subspace keypair — anyone can join by generating one) vs **Owned** namespaces (one owner keypair controls everything and grants subspace capabilities to others). This maps cleanly to "open community" vs "managed team."
- **Maturity:** Spec is mature and stable; willow-rs is an active implementation. No member *removal* primitive beyond capability expiry/restriction — revocation is weak.

#### MLS (RFC 9420) / MLS Architecture (RFC 9750) / Decentralized MLS
- **MLS:** The IETF standard for group key agreement (TreeKEM-based), strong FS + PCS. RFC 9420 (July 2023). **RFC 9750** (the architecture, April 2025) notes the **Delivery Service and Authentication Service can be abstract** — which is the hook for P2P use.
- **The P2P problem:** MLS *requires* a Delivery Service to totally-order Commits. In a decentralised setting you must retain key material to process Commits out of order, which violates MLS's deletion schedule and **significantly reduces forward secrecy**.
- **Decentralized MLS (DMLS):** `draft-kohbrok-mls-dmls` (Konrad Kohbrok, Phoenix R&D), now at **-03 (20 Oct 2025)**, Informational, based on **FREEK** (Fork-Resilient CGKA, Alwen/Mularczyk/Tselekounis, ePrint 2023/394). It "punctures" the init secret of old epochs so retaining old state is *safer* (though still not as safe as deletion). A separate `draft-xue-mls-decentralized` catalogues distributed uses. **Status: individual Internet-Drafts, not adopted RFCs.**
- **Fit:** If you want a standards-track, formally-analysed CGKA, MLS is it — but the P2P story is unfinished drafts, and the FS erosion is real.

#### Delta Chat SecureJoin + chatmail
- **What it is:** A shipping, mass-market E2EE messenger over ordinary email (Autocrypt + SecureJoin over OpenPGP/rPGP). Guaranteed E2EE since v1.42; **green checkmark** = verified.
- **Invites (the UX benchmark):** SecureJoin requires **only one out-of-band user action** — scanning a QR code (or opening an invite link). The invite code carries the inviter's key fingerprint + contact info; single-use limits QR exposure; an optional short-code confirmation protects against QR leakage. SecureJoin **v3** hides cryptographic identities. Note the deliberate "business card vs verification" QR-use-case tension.
- **Adversary model:** Deliberately **ignores infiltrators/malicious members** and focuses on transport attackers (malicious servers/networks), because the web-of-trust mental model is too hard for users.
- **Formal analysis:** ETH Zurich (Yuanming Song, Lenka Mareková, Kenneth G. Paterson) — **ePrint 2024/918** / USENIX Security 2024 — described **five new attacks** exploiting cross-protocol interactions between SecureJoin and Autocrypt plus rPGP bugs (including a gossip-key injection issue). Delta Chat fixed **20 identified issues** in **v1.44** (patches submitted 20 Feb 2024 and reviewed by the researchers before public disclosure on 10 June 2024).
- **Fit:** The gold standard for *usable* invite/verification flows and an honest, narrow threat model. Not a library you'd embed, but the pattern to copy.

#### Matrix (state resolution v2, Element)
- **What it is:** Federated (not P2P) group messaging. Membership/power-levels are room state resolved via **State Resolution v2**.
- **Adversary work:** Element's Kegan Dougal authored **ERA: Epoch-Resolved Arbitration for Duelling Admins** (arXiv **2601.22963**, PaPoC 2026). Key argument: in group-management CRDTs, the materialised view can "roll back" concurrently, and a **Byzantine admin can exploit concurrency to win a mutual-revocation duel**. ERA adds optional **epoch events** emitted by a **finality arbiter** in response to non-monotonic operations (e.g. demote), giving a **bounded total order and finality** while preserving availability — explicitly analogised to blockchain finality (rollbacks possible until finalised).
- **Related hardening:** Matrix's 2025 "Hydra" federation-security project (with KIT's Florian Jacob, disclosed Aug 2025) and earlier practically-exploitable Matrix crypto findings (Albrecht/Celi/Dowling/Jones, S&P 2023) are the cautionary tales for rolling your own.

#### iroh (n0)
- **What it is:** A Rust "dial-any-device" QUIC transport/discovery library (NodeId = ed25519 public key; hole-punching + relay fallback; pkarr/DHT/DNS discovery). Very active (0.9x series in 2026; tickets moved to their own `iroh-tickets` crate).
- **Invites/addressing:** **NodeTicket / NodeAddr** — a serialisable blob with a NodeId and optional relay/direct addresses. Since 0.15 the default ticket is just the NodeId (shorter).
- **Authorisation:** **iroh provides transport, not authorisation.** Their own maintainers (Discussion #3168) tell you: knowing a NodeId is enough to attempt a connection, so if discovery is enabled a leaked NodeId is a connection-authz issue; you must **keep your own allow-list of trusted public keys** or layer mTLS/capabilities on top. This is the clean seam where you'd bolt on @localfirst/auth-style membership or UCAN.
- **Fit:** Exactly the transport layer to pair with a separate membership layer; complements Commonware.

#### Holepunch Pear / Hypercore / Keet
- **What it is:** A P2P app platform: Hypercore (signed append-only logs), Hyperswarm (DHT discovery), **Autobase** (multi-writer data structures). Keet is the flagship chat/video app.
- **Invites (Keet's pattern):** **`blind-pairing-core`** — `createInvite(key)` yields an invite + public key; a **CandidateRequest** is encoded and sent, the existing member opens it as a **MemberRequest** using the invite public key, confirms, and the candidate is admitted. "Blind" pairing means the DHT/relay learns nothing about who's pairing. This is the closest production analogue to a single-use, second-channel invite seed.
- **Membership/multi-writer:** Via Autobase; multi-device is native.
- **Maturity:** Production (Keet ships). JavaScript/Node + Bare runtime. Backed by Tether/Bitfinex.

#### AT Protocol / Bluesky (contrasting *federated* model)
- **Identity:** `did:plc` identifiers in the **PLC directory**; a DID document lists **rotation keys** (secp256k1/p256, priority-ordered) and signing keys.
- **Recovery/migration:** Rotation keys are the account-recovery mechanism — a user-held rotation key enables an **"adversarial migration"** (moving your account even if your PDS/host is hostile), and there's a recovery time window plus a "tombstone" operation. Handles are indirection over DIDs. Identity operations require an out-of-band email token as a second factor.
- **Lesson for you:** This is the cleanest *shipping* example of "user-held rotation key as recovery," and of separating identity from hosting — but it depends on a (self-certifying but centrally-operated) PLC directory, i.e. it's federated, not P2P.

#### Newer / commercial local-first entrants (2025–2026)
- **Jazz (jazz.tools / Garden Computing):** Production (`jazz-tools` v0.20.18, mid-Aug 2026, MIT). Data = "CoValues" owned by **Groups** or Accounts (account-ownership being deprecated). Fixed roles: **reader / writer / admin / manager / writeOnly** plus a `revoked` role and per-role invite roles; **no custom roles**; "most permissive role wins"; groups can nest with role inheritance (writeOnly not inherited). Invites = `addMember(account, role)` or shareable **invite links** (work for unauthenticated users) — but **"invites do not expire and cannot be revoked."** Permissions enforced **client-side** (unauthorised transactions silently dropped); revocation rotates the read key (old ciphertext stays readable). Auth: **passkey, passphrase (BIP39-style recovery phrase), Clerk, Better Auth**; no server-side reset for the serverless methods. A Rust relational rewrite exists but is alpha.
- **Ditto (ditto.live):** P2P mesh sync but **auth is server-mediated** — devices must authenticate to Ditto Server (central CA) before joining the mesh; permissions centrally managed and cert-enforced by all peers. Its **`SharedKey`/offline** mode explicitly warns: "A compromised device can read and re-distribute the key, and there is no method to unauthorize that key in the future." (Do not confuse with the unrelated Eclipse Ditto IoT project.)
- **Zero / Rocicorp:** **Explicitly not local-first and not P2P** — "a client-server system with an authoritative server." Permissions are server-authoritative (custom queries/mutators with a JWT-derived context, or an older `definePermissions` row-based ruleset). Included as a boundary case: if your clients tolerate a trusted server, this is dramatically simpler.
- **Evolu:** Local-first E2EE SQLite+CRDT; identity/recovery via a **BIP39 mnemonic** (the mnemonic *is* the recovery). Evolu 8 introduces AppOwner/ShardOwner. In production inside Trezor Suite, but the team explicitly does **not yet recommend it to a general audience**; passkey-backed local auth and multi-owner coordinated deletion are **planned, not shipped**.
- **Fireproof:** E2EE content-addressed ledger with a **shared symmetric key**; no built-in identity/roles — sharing/auth is delegated to the app (web3.storage connector uses UCAN). No fine-grained membership revocation in core.
- **Automerge Repo:** Auth story is still the `@localfirst/auth` adapter aspiration plus Keyhive/Beelay as the future direction; not a finished built-in.

#### Intellectual ancestors
- **Keybase sigchain + Per-User Keychain (PUK):** The pattern several systems descend from. A public, append-only, hash-chained **sigchain** records device add/revoke (its Merkle roots historically anchored into the Bitcoin blockchain); a **PUK** is rolled on every device revocation — the revoking device generates a new PUK, encrypts it for all remaining devices, and writes the revocation to the chain. Old ciphertext stays readable to a compromised old key; future writes are protected. Recovery via **paper key**. (Now maintenance-mode post-Zoom acquisition.)
- **Secure Scuttlebutt:** Private groups via `ssb-tribes`/box2/envelope (shared symmetric group key). The base design had **no member removal**; the later **`ssb-group-exclusion-spec`** adds **epochs** — create a new epoch key shared only to "remaining members," excluding the removed party. Same key-rotation-on-revoke pattern. Low-maintenance ecosystem as of 2026.
- **Nostr:** Identity = a single secp256k1 keypair; **NIP-29** groups are **relay-authoritative** (the relay enforces membership/roles via kinds 9000–9009/39000–39003; there is "no way to create a group" independent of a relay). The infamous limitation: **no key recovery** — lose your nsec and the account is gone; mitigations (NIP-46 remote signers, NIP-49 encrypted keys, NIP-06 BIP39 seeds) are prevention, not recovery.

### B. The five concerns, cross-cut

#### 1. Invitations
- **The dominant usable pattern** is a **single-use secret seed/token delivered over a second channel**, where the token carries enough to bootstrap mutual authentication. Concrete instances: @localfirst/auth's **Seitan tokens** (asymmetric-PAKE), Keet's **blind-pairing** invites, Delta Chat's **SecureJoin QR** (one out-of-band action, fingerprint embedded, single-use, optional short-code), iroh's **NodeTicket**, Jazz's **invite links**.
- **UX cost ranking (lowest to highest friction):** QR scan with embedded fingerprint (Delta Chat) < invite link (Jazz, single action but weaker binding) < blind-pairing request/confirm (Keet, two actions) < manual fingerprint/safety-number comparison (Signal — measured as nearly unusable; see UX section).
- **The critical pitfall is invite-link leakage.** Links in chat histories, logs, or screenshots are a real compromise vector. Mitigations: single-use, short expiry, a confirmation step by an existing member (Keet's `MemberRequest.confirm`), and never granting standing capability from the link alone. **Jazz's "invites never expire and can't be revoked" is a concrete anti-pattern** to avoid copying.

#### 2. Revocation
- **Distinguish write-revocation from read-revocation.** Write-revocation (stop accepting a member's signed operations) is a membership-log/authz concern and is comparatively easy: record a signed removal, converge. Read-revocation (stop them decrypting *future* content) requires **key rotation** (CGKA/PCS) — and **cannot claw back already-decrypted data**. Every mature system accepts this: Keybase PUK, Jazz read-key rotation, SSB epochs, BeeKEM PCS all leave old ciphertext readable to the removed party.
- **Propagation latency/guarantees vary sharply.** UCAN: eventually consistent, CID-based, "last line of defence," so lean on expiry. Signature-chain systems (@localfirst/auth, p2panda): revocation converges as fast as the log syncs, with a deterministic resolver deciding concurrent conflicts. BeeKEM: PCS on the next Update, causal-order-based.
- **The hard case is concurrent/duelling revocation.** p2panda's default **"strong removal"** resolver and ERA's **epoch finality** are the two documented answers. Pure last-writer-wins CRDT semantics are *insecure* here.

#### 3. Membership management (who may add/remove, concurrency)
- **The central irreversible choice: free delegation vs manager-only.** Keyhive/UCAN/Meadowcap-communal = free delegation (any capable member delegates onward), maximal flexibility, hardest to reason about. p2panda (manager-only, strong removal), Meadowcap-owned, Jazz (admins/managers with fixed rules) = constrained, easier to audit. For an enterprise team app, **manager-only with an explicit role lattice is the safer default**.
- **A clean, stealable role lattice:** p2panda's **Pull / Read / Write / Manage** (note Pull ≠ Read — replicate-without-decrypt is a genuinely useful capability for relays/backup nodes). Jazz's **reader/writer/admin/manager/writeOnly** is a richer variant.
- **Devices as first-class members vs users-with-devices** is another up-front decision. Keybase's model (device keys never leave the device; users are sets of devices unified by a PUK) is the reference; @localfirst/auth follows it ("device keys generated locally, never leave the device"). Deciding late forces a painful data-model migration.
- **Concurrency resolution should be a pluggable, explicitly-chosen policy** (p2panda's `Resolver` trait is the model): "strong removal," "freeze on conflict," or "epoch-finalised" — not an accident of merge order.

#### 4. Credential / account recovery
- **Primary answer everywhere: multi-device.** If keys live on ≥2 devices, losing one is not a recovery event. This is the honest baseline and should be your default.
- **Beyond that, the spectrum:**
  - **Centralised, hardened (baseline to beat):** **Signal SVR3** (ePrint 2024/887, OSDI 2024, Connell/Fang/Schmidt/Dauterman/Popa) — Argon2-stretch a PIN into an auth token + a master-key input; split trust across **three heterogeneous enclaves on three clouds** (Intel SGX/Azure, AMD SEV-SNP/GCP, AWS Nitro), so compromising two clouds still fails; SGX-style rate-limiting defeats offline brute force; **no PIN recovery** by design. The paper states "SVR3 costs $0.0025/user/year and takes 365ms for a user to recover their key," with capacity for over 500 million users (note: the Berkeley SKY project page lists a revised $0.0015/user/year and 321 ms — verify which figure before quoting externally).
  - **Decentralised threshold (the P2P-native option):** **Kintsugi** (Emilie Ma & Martin Kleppmann, arXiv 2507.21122). Password-authenticated **threshold-OPRF + DPSS**: a threshold **t+1** of recovery nodes help decrypt a shared backup; tolerates **t** honest-but-curious colluders and **n−t−1** offline nodes; **no special hardware**; recovery nodes can be **contacts' devices**, acting as a "social rate limit" on guessing. Rust + libp2p + Tauri; explicitly a **research preview, not production-ready**.
  - **Passkey-derived keys (WebAuthn PRF):** Derive an encryption key from a synced passkey via the **PRF extension**. Shipping in Safari 18/iOS 18 for **platform** authenticators; used by WhatsApp backups, 1Password, and Moxie's Confer. **Two serious caveats:** (a) Apple does **not** pass extension data to/from **roaming** authenticators (YubiKeys) on iOS/iPadOS — a platform-level gap; (b) WebAuthn spec co-editor Tim Cappalli publicly warns *against* using PRF to derive encryption keys, because a lost authenticator means permanently lost data — turning a replaceable credential into non-replaceable key material.
  - **Self-custody mnemonic:** BIP39 (Evolu, Jazz passphrase, Nostr NIP-06). Simple, no trust in others, but pure user responsibility.
  - **User-held rotation key:** AT Protocol's model — recovery/migration key the user controls.
  - **No recovery:** Nostr nsec, Fireproof-if-key-lost. Acceptable only for specific threat models.
- **The 2026 comparative framework:** Funke & Gellert, **"A Comparative Evaluation of End-to-End-Encrypted Key Retrieval in Backup Protocols"** (IACR **ePrint 2026/1108**, June 2026), evaluates recovery-code, deployed (SVR/WBP), and academic schemes across **usability, deployability, and security**, making the hardware-dependency and complex-primitive trust costs explicit. This is the reference for choosing.

#### 5. Robust protection from adversaries
- **Transport vs infiltrator adversaries — choose your scope explicitly.** Delta Chat's deliberate decision to defend only against transport attackers (malicious servers/networks) and *ignore* malicious members is the pragmatic mainstream choice, precisely because usable identity assurance is unsolved.
- **Sybil resistance is a data-model property, not a feature you add.** Kleppmann & Howard, **"Byzantine Eventual Consistency and the Fundamental Limits of Peer-to-Peer Databases"** (arXiv 2012.00472): a class of applications is *inherently* Sybil-immune because they tolerate arbitrary numbers of Byzantine nodes — you get this by making convergence depend only on cryptographically-authored operations, not on node counts/voting. Kleppmann's "Making CRDTs Byzantine Fault Tolerant" (PaPoC 2022) gives the Sybil-immune convergence construction.
- **Cross-fork attacks** are the decentralised-specific CGKA threat: Causal TreeKEM and Weidner et al.'s DCGKA are vulnerable; **BeeKEM's cross-fork security** (formalised in ePrint 2026/1434) is the current answer, parameterised by κ.
- **Duelling-admin / rollback attacks** (ERA, arXiv 2601.22963): a Byzantine admin exploits concurrency to win a mutual revocation; mitigate with strong-removal resolvers or epoch finality.
- **Formal foundations to lean on:** Weidner/Kleppmann/Hugenroth/Beresford **DCGKA** (CCS 2021, ePrint 2020/1281) defines the core decentralised primitive; FREEK (ePrint 2023/394) underpins DMLS; TreeSync/formal-MLS work underpins RFC 9420. The requester's brief also cites Rault/Ignat/Perrin (CRDT-based access control), Jacob & Hartenstein (hash chronicles / Matrix), extend-only directed posets / Byzantine-tolerant RDTs (arXiv 2304.04318), and "Towards System-Oriented Formal Verification of Local-First Access Control" (arXiv 2604.23560) — I was unable to independently re-verify these four in this pass, so treat the specific IDs as from the brief rather than confirmed here, though they are consistent with the surrounding literature.

### C. The human-factors reality
- **Fingerprint/safety-number comparison is empirically close to unusable.** In Schröder et al.'s study, from a sample of 28 computer-science students, **21 were unable to successfully verify their recipient's public key** in Signal. Vaziripour et al.'s "Is that you, Alice?" (2017) found that **only 14%** of participants figured out how to perform the authentication ceremony on their own; even after being told to do so, 79% succeeded but it "took them 3.2 minutes to start the authentication ceremony and an additional 4.6 minutes to complete it, on average" (both summarised in Sequoia-PGP's "Hey Signal! Great Encryption Needs Great Authentication," 28 June 2021). A long-standing Signal-iOS bug report documents users — including frequent ones — misreading a *failed* verification as success because the success/failure feedback colour is easy to miss. **Do not build a product whose security depends on users comparing hex strings.**
- **The industry response is to remove the human from the loop where possible.** **Signal Automatic Key Verification** launched **11 Aug 2026**, using a **key-transparency log** to auto-verify that the server handed out the right key, showing a green check + "Encryption verified." Signal engineer Katherine Yen framed it as: *"Signal is always end-to-end encrypted, and automatic key verification provides an additional, streamlined way to confirm that there's no unexpected party between you and the other 'end' of an end-to-end encrypted session"* (Help Net Security, 12 Aug 2026). The log is audited by **Cloudflare and Trail of Bits** — Trail of Bits "implemented their Signal auditor from scratch, based on Signal's specification rather than its reference implementation" and operates it unpaid "as a public good" (Tech Times, 12 Aug 2026); Cloudflare has run the same infrastructure for WhatsApp since September 2024 and built Signal's auditor "in Rust on its own Workers platform, leaning on an append-only verification method from Meta's open-source key directory library" (The Next Web, 12 Aug 2026). Crucially, it does **not** verify real-world identity: *"It proves the key belongs to that identifier, not that the identifier belongs to the person you think. You also need your contact's phone number. If they use username-only discovery, automatic verification is unavailable."*
- **What mainstream apps actually ship, and their known problems:**
  - Signal safety numbers / auto-verification (per-conversation numbers to avoid stale-fingerprint confusion; still doesn't stop impersonation).
  - WhatsApp security codes; iMessage Contact Key Verification (key-transparency based); Element/Matrix cross-signing + emoji SAS; Delta Chat green checkmarks (QR-first).
  - The recurring finding: **users prefer "registration"/directory models over manual key exchange** even at reduced security ("good enough"), and **QR scanning is the only verification ceremony with acceptable completion rates.**
- **Concrete UI/UX patterns worth adopting:** QR-first invite/verification with embedded fingerprint (Delta Chat); one out-of-band action maximum; green-check/"verified" affordance with an unmissable *failure* state; framing recovery as "add a second device now" rather than "write down 24 words"; never surfacing raw keys/CIDs to end users.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Decide the irreversibles first (before any code).**
1. **Forward-secrecy vs concurrency tolerance.** For a team-collaboration app where members go offline for weeks, you *need* concurrency tolerance, which means accepting reduced forward secrecy (BeeKEM κ>1, or DMLS's retained/punctured state). Decide this explicitly and document that "delete means future-only" for read-revocation. Threshold to revisit: if you later target high-risk users (journalists/activists) who need strong FS, you'd have to re-architect.
2. **Membership authority model.** Choose **manager-only with an explicit role lattice** (adopt p2panda's Pull/Read/Write/Manage) unless you have a specific reason to want free delegation. This is hard to reverse because it shapes every authz check.
3. **Devices as first-class members.** Adopt the Keybase/@localfirst/auth model: per-device keys generated locally, never exported; a user is a set of devices. This makes multi-device recovery and per-device revocation natural.
4. **Concurrency-resolution policy** as an explicit, pluggable choice (strong-removal default).

**Stage 1 — Minimal viable design (build now, Rust/Commonware-friendly).**
- **Membership/authz:** A signed, hash-chained membership log — conceptually @localfirst/auth's signature chain, but in Rust use **`p2panda-auth`** (production-track, MIT/Apache, the `Resolver` trait, four-level access). This is your best-matched shipping Rust component today.
- **Transport/discovery:** **iroh** (NodeId/NodeTicket), with your own **allow-list of trusted member public keys** derived from the membership log — do not rely on ticket possession alone for authorisation. This slots beside Commonware and your friend's "frame" format.
- **Invites:** Single-use, short-expiry, second-channel seed carrying the inviter's fingerprint, with an existing-member confirmation step. Model the flow on Delta Chat SecureJoin (QR-first) and Keet blind-pairing. Explicitly make invites expirable and revocable (learn from Jazz's mistake).
- **Read-encryption:** Start with a simple per-group symmetric key with **rotation on membership change** (the SSB-epoch / Keybase-PUK pattern). Only adopt a full CGKA when you need frequent PCS.
- **Recovery:** **Multi-device as primary.** Fallback = BIP39 passphrase (self-custody) and/or passkey — but if you use WebAuthn PRF, treat Cappalli's warning as binding: never let a single passkey be the *only* thing standing between the user and their data.
- **Identity assurance / moderation / spam:** Declare out of scope for the crypto layer; handle at the application/social layer (approval by an existing manager, rate limits, reporting). Set this expectation with enterprise stakeholders on day one.

**Stage 2 — Upgrade paths (adopt as they mature).**
- Swap the simple key rotation for **BeeKEM** (`beekem` crate) once it has a security audit and stabler APIs — it's the best conceptual match and it's Rust. Track Ink & Switch lab notes past 06 and ePrint 2026/1434.
- Consider **DMLS** if you want standards-track/formally-analysed CGKA and are willing to follow an unfinished draft (currently -03).
- Add **Kintsugi**-style threshold recovery (contacts' devices as recovery nodes) if/when it hardens past research-preview — it's the P2P-native answer to "lost everything," and it's Rust + libp2p.
- Add **key-transparency-based auto-verification** (à la Signal Aug 2026) if you ever run any directory/relay, to remove manual fingerprint comparison.

**Benchmarks that would change these recommendations:**
- If Keyhive/BeeKEM ships a security audit + stable API → promote it from Stage 2 to Stage 1 as the fused membership+encryption layer.
- If your clients will accept a trusted coordination server → reconsider whether you need P2P authz at all; **Zero/Rocicorp or Ditto** collapse most of this complexity, and even MLS-with-a-DS becomes viable.
- If you must guarantee strong forward secrecy → drop concurrency tolerance (accept online-only membership changes) and use standard MLS.

## Caveats
- **Maturity is the dominant risk.** The designs that best fit your goals (Keyhive/BeeKEM) are **explicitly pre-alpha, unaudited, with unstable APIs**; Kintsugi and the Rust Jazz rewrite are research/alpha; DMLS and `draft-xue` are individual Internet-Drafts, not adopted standards. The production-ready options either accept a trusted server (Ditto, Zero, Matrix) or make deliberate simplifications (Delta Chat ignores infiltrators; Jazz invites can't be revoked; Ditto SharedKey can't unauthorise a key).
- **Read-revocation never claws back already-decrypted data** in any of these systems — this is fundamental, not a limitation of a particular implementation.
- **Dates/versions cited are as of early September 2026** and this space moves fast (iroh ships breaking releases frequently; p2panda-auth was at 0.6.0; jazz-tools at 0.20.18). Verify current versions before committing.
- **Four academic references from the brief (Rault/Ignat/Perrin; Jacob & Hartenstein; arXiv 2304.04318; arXiv 2604.23560) I relayed without independent re-verification in this pass** — confirm the exact IDs/authors before citing formally. Similarly, the SVR3 cost/latency figure differs between the paper ($0.0025/365 ms) and the Berkeley SKY project page ($0.0015/321 ms).
- **"P2P" is used loosely across sources.** Several "decentralised" products (Ditto, Nostr NIP-29, AT Protocol, Matrix) rely on a trusted or semi-trusted coordination point (server, relay, PLC directory). Genuinely serverless authorisation is the exception (Keyhive, p2panda, @localfirst/auth, SSB), and it's exactly the hardest case.
- **What these systems do NOT give you, restated for stakeholders:** identity assurance (that a member is the human they claim), moderation/abuse handling, and spam resistance. In practice P2P products handle these socially (manager approval, invite-gating, reporting) or by leaning on an out-of-band trust channel — not cryptographically.
